Abstract

Question. With a fixed weekly review capacity, which content pages should a strategist look at first — and can a model beat a transparent rule at ranking them?

Data. An anonymized sample of 30,000 content items across 32 pseudonymous FlyRank clients, one trailing 90-day search-performance snapshot per page.

Method. A transparent CTR-vs-position baseline rule was compared against a Random Forest trained on a leakage-checked feature set, evaluated on a client-grouped holdout — zero clients shared between train and test.

Result. The model lifts precision among the top 20 flagged pages from 0.70 to 0.75, and whole-list ranking quality (ROC-AUC) from 0.596 to 0.708 — a real, moderate improvement over an already-competitive rule, not a dramatic reinvention of it.

What it's for. A decision-support ranking for a content reviewer's queue — directional and observed, never a causal or predictive claim about search rankings, and never a substitute for a human's final call on any one page.

1 · Introduction & problem

A backlog no reviewer can clear by working harder

In the sample used for this project, 54.2% of pages are trending down. Of those declining pages, 80.9% still carry real demand (90-day impressions ≥ 100) — most aren't dead traffic, they're at-risk traffic. A content strategist reviewing 20 pages a week would need roughly 658 weeks to clear today's declining set alone; at 50 a week, still 263 weeks. Reviewer capacity cannot keep pace with the backlog — so which pages get looked at first is not a detail, it's the whole decision.

Who acts: a content strategist or SEO editor with a capped weekly review budget (this paper uses 20 and 50 as illustrative capacities). Cost of a wrong call: a false positive burns one of a reviewer's limited slots on a page that didn't need it — a bounded, recoverable cost. A false negative lets a page with real demand keep losing visibility, unnoticed, until the next cycle — a compounding one. Because the scarce resource is reviewer attention, this is a ranking problem, and precision@K is the metric that matches the actual decision — not raw accuracy.

A first check of eight candidate signals against the outcome (position, impressions, staleness, word count, engagement, content age, scroll rate, AI-traffic share) found every correlation close to zero (max |r| ≈ 0.05) — no single column, or an obvious one-line combination, cleanly separates decliners from the rest. That's the shape a learned model is built for: many weak, tangled signals worth combining, not a pattern a person can write as one rule. Whether a model actually earns its complexity over a transparent rule, though, is an empirical question — not assumed here, and tested directly in the sections below.

2 · Data

One anonymized snapshot, thirty-two clients, no client-identifying fields

This paper's results are built entirely on data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv, an anonymized sample release shipped inside the project's own repository — no gated access or credentials required to rerun it. Grain: one row per content item, per client, as of one trailing 90-day snapshot. Scope: 30,000 content items across 32 pseudonymous clients (client_id / content_id are opaque hashes, used only for grouping and joins — never as features).

Deliberately excluded, and why:

  • Any FlyRank product-computed decision field (health_score, priority_score, action_type) — not shipped in this release, and would be circular if they were: feeding a system's own decision into a model meant to discover priority just teaches it to copy that decision.
  • Raw client names, domains, URLs, page titles, or search queries — never shipped to interns, and excluded from every output this project produces.

A separate, exploratory data track. Earlier in this project I also queried FlyRank's full production warehouse (a ~9.8M-row daily panel for one mid-2026 month, via gated Hugging Face access) to test whether a genuine forward-observed label — did a page's demand actually fall in the 28 days after a decision date — was buildable at all. It is (a small, honest 5-feature model scored ROC-AUC 0.775 on that construction), but reproducing it requires requesting warehouse access and isn't something a reader can rerun from the public repo alone. The results in this paper use the fully public, no-credentials-needed CSV instead, which trades that forward-looking rigor for full reproducibility — a trade-off named explicitly in Limitations, not glossed over.

3 · Methodology

Label, baseline, model, split — and the leakage hunt

Label

is_declining_label = trend_direction == "down", a bucket derived from trend_pct (itself a comparison of the most recent 30 days of impressions against the prior 30). This is an observed, backward-looking proxy for decline, not a future-observed outcome — that gap is named honestly and revisited in Limitations.

Baseline: a transparent CTR-vs-position rule

A page is worth review if it holds a real position, gets real traffic, and its click-through rate falls short of what pages at the same position tier typically earn. Signal-testing two candidate rules first: staleness (days since last update — the assumption behind refresh-tier flags) came back MIXED, not monotonic; CTR vs. position came back CONFIRMED, falling cleanly from 2.71% at top-3 to 0.15% at deep positions. The rule is built on the confirmed signal only, encoded directly — no fitted weights: score = visible × reachable × ctr_gap × log1p(impressions_90d), with the tier-mean CTR lookup fit on training data only.

Model & feature set

Random Forest, chosen over Logistic Regression by a narrow, contested margin — on an earlier full-feature comparison the two were close enough (Logistic Regression actually edged ahead on precision@100 and average precision) that Random Forest's extra complexity was not an obvious win. It is used here because it produced the strongest result on the final, leakage-checked feature set, not because it dominated on every cut of the data — a claim the paper is careful not to overstate.

The feature set deliberately excludes impressions_90d, clicks_90d, ctr, and every other trailing-90-day engagement column. Features used: avg_position, content_age_days, days_since_last_update, word_count, char_count, search_volume, competition, cpc, plus content-type/intent/provider metadata and missingness flags.

Validation design

An 80/20 client-grouped split — 6 of 32 clients held out entirely, so zero clients appear in both train and test. This matters: a naive random row-level split (tested directly, as a deliberate before/after) let 31 of 32 clients appear in both sides, and inflated precision@20 to 0.95 versus the honest 0.70 — the model was partly memorizing each client's own base decline rate, not learning to generalize to a client it had never scored. That gap is itself a finding, not just a caveat.

Leakage checks

Three checks, not one

1. The classic trap. Deliberately adding the label's own future-window input as a feature drove AUC to 1.000, exactly the leakage confession one would expect — then it was removed.

2. A subtler window-overlap check. impressions_90d correlates at r = 0.98 with the exact columns the label is computed from — a structural overlap, not a copy. An ablation (with vs. without the overlapping features) moved ROC-AUC by only −0.017 and improved precision@k — real enough to disclose and exclude, but not the dramatic "collapse toward 0.7 from near-1.0" a classic leakage case would show.

3. A harness sanity check. Smuggling trend_pct in as the only feature reproduced AUC = 1.000 — confirming the audit would actually catch real leakage if it were present, which is what makes check #2's more moderate result trustworthy rather than a blind spot.

Three honest reads, not one flattering headline:

  • The baseline rule is a genuinely strong top-of-list ranker. Using nothing but three trailing-90-day numbers multiplied together, it is not far behind the model at precision@20 and @100. A learned model decisively beating a well-built rule was not guaranteed, and at the very top of the queue it mostly didn't happen by a wide margin.
  • The model's real advantage is breadth, not a single top-of-list blowout. ROC-AUC (+0.112) and average precision (+0.074) show it separates decliners from non-decliners better across the whole ranked list — it picks up a broader mix of signal (position, content age, tier, content metadata) the three-input rule never looks at.
  • Precision@100 barely moved (0.65 → 0.66). The model's advantage concentrates near the top of the queue, exactly where reviewer capacity is scarcest and matters most — but that also means "the model wins everywhere" would overstate the finding.
5 · Limitations & honest framing

What this work cannot claim

  • The label is a backward-looking proxy, not a future-observed outcome. is_declining_label buckets a 60-day trailing comparison at export time — it does not answer "will this page decline in the next 30 days," only "was this page already trending down as of this snapshot." A genuine forward-window label was shown to be buildable from the full warehouse (ROC-AUC 0.775 in exploratory work) but requires gated access this paper's reproducible pipeline does not depend on.
  • No time-based holdout. Validation here tests generalization to unseen clients within one snapshot, never to future data. Seasonal effects, algorithm updates, or drift over time are untested.
  • No revenue data. The recommendation queue's "value-at-stake" number is a directional, order-of-magnitude proxy from CPC and estimated recoverable clicks — not a dollar forecast.
  • A residual, disclosed leakage risk. impressions_90d-family features structurally overlap the label's own defining window (r = 0.98) and are excluded from the model for exactly that reason — even though the ablation test found their effect on the score was modest, not dominant.
  • The no_position_data subgroup (4.0% of pages) is a measurement gap, not a clean bill of health. These pages show a 0.7% decline rate versus 56.4% for pages with real position data — almost certainly an artifact of near-zero traffic (a swing from 1 impression to 0 reads as a huge relative "decline" on almost nothing), not evidence they're safe.
  • No causal claims, anywhere. Nothing here says a refresh causes recovery, or describes Google's ranking algorithm — this is an association/prediction study on FlyRank's own cross-sectional traffic data, and every claim in this paper uses observed / measured / directional / decision-support language on purpose.
  • An internship-scale demonstration project. Nothing here should be presented to a client, or used for pricing, staffing, or contractual claims, without new, purpose-built validation.
6 · Ranked recommendations

A five-action queue, from two signals that stay separate on purpose

The playbook combines two validated signals without blending them into one opaque score: the descriptive CTR-vs-position rule (today's underperformance vs. peers) and the predictive risk model (safe features only). Every page gets a fixed reason code and one of five actions — never a bare, unexplained score.

Seven human-readable archetypes give the same logic a name a reviewer can act on without re-deriving it: champion (578)hidden_gem (8,107)ctr_watch (3,859)steady_performer (8,751)quiet_decliner (2,408)at_risk_visible (5,092)data_quality_gap (1,205)

What a reviewer checks before acting

  1. Read the reason code(s) and archetype — never act on rank or score alone.
  2. For a flagged decline: confirm it's real, not an artifact of near-zero traffic (the same failure mode found in the model's most confident wrong calls — all three shared 1–3 impressions in the whole 90-day window).
  3. For a CTR flag: check whether the page's title/meta matches query intent before touching content — a CTR gap can be a metadata problem, with a different fix than a content problem.
  4. no_action_insufficient_data pages go to whoever owns tracking/tagging for that client, not to a content reviewer.
What should never be automated
  • No auto-publishing or auto-editing — the output is a queue to look at, never edits to apply.
  • No auto-pausing a client's content based on this score without human sign-off.
  • No client-facing or contractual claims from these numbers — precision@k describes this project's own held-out test on one anonymized snapshot, not a validated SLA.
  • No treating no_position_data rows as low-risk just because the model (correctly, given the data) scores them that way.
7 · Reproducibility

Rerun it from a clean clone

Everything in this paper traces back to committed code and receipts in the public repo:

  • Data: data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv (ships in the repo, no credentials needed).
  • Notebooks, in order: Week 1 — question, Week 2 — task framing, Week 3 — data contract, Week 4 — baseline & signal tests, Week 5 — model, Week 6 — validation & leakage audit, Week 7 — action playbook, Week 8 — capstone synthesis (this paper).
  • Metrics receipts (committed, not regenerated by hand): work/outputs/action_playbook_metrics.json.
  • Figures: work/figures/action_mix.svg, work/figures/decay_refresh_insight.svg.
  • Fixed random seed (42) throughout every split and model fit.
